Everything in this document is assembled from six well-established findings about how memory works, plus the practices that strong candidates and DSA mentors converge on independently. Understanding why makes you far more likely to hold the line when a session feels unpleasant — and the effective ones do.

## 4.1 The six principles

| Principle | The finding | What you do differently |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Retrieval practice | Attempting to produce an answer from memory strengthens it far more than re-reading — even when the attempt fails. | Never open the old solution before re-attempting. Struggle the full time-box first. |
| Spacing | The same minutes spread across weeks beat the same minutes in one day, dramatically, for long-term recall. | Touch at day 1, 3, 7, 21 — not four times on Saturday. |
| Interleaving | Mixing types within a session hurts in-session performance and helps later transfer. | After repair, problems go into a mixed pool with the labels stripped. |
| Desirable difficulty | If it feels smooth, it isn't working. Effortful retrieval is the signal. | Cruising through a re-solve means that problem is done — swap in a harder one. |
| Generation effect | Predicting before seeing improves retention of the correct answer, even when the prediction is wrong. | Write your guessed approach and complexity before opening any editorial. |
| Elaborative interrogation | "Why does this work?" and "what breaks if I remove this line?" build far more durable structure than the line itself. | For every template, know the one boundary that breaks it. |

## 4.2 What strong candidates actually do

- **Revise by pattern, not by problem.** Nobody re-solves 111 problems. They repair 19 templates and the problems come back attached. This is the single largest efficiency multiplier available to you and it is the backbone of Part II.
- **Keep a mistake log, not a solution log.** Solutions are on the internet. Your specific recurring errors are not. "I forget the third dedup in 3Sum" is worth more than any editorial you will ever read.
- **Anchor problems.** 2–3 per pattern that ARE the pattern. Permanent rotation, forever. Everything else is derivable.
- **Re-solve until fluent, not until correct.** The bar is not "I got it." It is "I got it without hesitation, inside the bar, and I can explain the boundary conditions."
- **Explain out loud.** Speaking exposes gaps that silent reading hides completely. If you can't narrate it, you can't defend it while an interviewer watches you type.
- **Timed from day one.** Untimed practice trains a different skill than a 90-minute OA with three problems. Visible timer, every re-solve.
- **Blank-page the template weekly.** Not the problem — the skeleton. If you can write the monotonic stack loop from nothing in 90 seconds, every monotonic stack problem is a variation. If you can't, each one is a fresh crisis.
- **Constraints first, always.** n ≤ 20 means exponential is fine. n ≤ 10⁵ means O(n log n) is the target. The constraint tells you the algorithm class before you have any idea what the algorithm is. §9.2 has the full table.

## 4.3 The anti-list — stop doing these

| Feels productive | Actually produces | Do instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Re-reading old solutions | Recognition, false confidence | Cold re-solve with the tab closed |
| Watching a video on a solved topic | Comfort | Blank-page the template, then check |
| Building a new tracker or plan | Displacement wearing a costume | Run one Part II card today |
| Three new problems in one pattern | Three more 20% rows | Two old cold re-solves + one new |
| Reading editorials 'to understand deeply' | Passive familiarity | Predict first, read second, close, rebuild |
| Sorting problems into more categories | The illusion of structure | The 60-second audit on 20 problems |
| Doing easy problems to move the count | A number that lies to you | Report L3+, not solved |
