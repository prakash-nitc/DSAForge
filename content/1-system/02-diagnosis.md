title: Diagnosis — why solved does not mean usable
---

You said you have forgotten many concepts. That is not a failure of intelligence or discipline; it is the predictable output of a specific method. Here is the mechanism, in four parts, because you cannot fix what you cannot name.

## 2.1 You trained recognition, not retrieval

A first solve — especially one with an editorial nearby — builds recognition memory: you can identify a solution when you see it. Interviews demand retrieval memory: producing it from a blank screen with nothing but the statement.

These are different systems with different decay curves. Recognition is cheap to form and decays into warm familiarity — "I've seen this." Retrieval is expensive to form and is the only thing that survives into a timed round. Every hour spent re-reading old solutions strengthens recognition and does almost nothing for retrieval. That is precisely why revision-by-reading feels productive and produces nothing.

## 2.2 You trained blocked; interviews are interleaved

You did Binary Search for a week, then Heap for a week. Inside a Binary Search week you never had to ask whether a problem was binary search — the calendar answered it. So you drilled the second half of the skill (execute the template) and never once drilled the first half (choose the template).

In an OA nothing is labelled. Blocked practice reliably produces higher in-session performance and reliably produces worse transfer. Interleaved practice feels worse while you do it and produces far better recall later. You have had eleven weeks of the version that feels good.

## 2.3 The forgetting curve is doing exactly what it does

One retrieval buys days. Two spaced retrievals buy weeks. Four buy months. You have been giving each problem one, which means every problem you solved in June is functionally gone — except worse than gone, because it is marked solved, so you skip it in revision and carry false confidence into the room.

**That false confidence is the single most dangerous thing in your current state.** It is what produces a candidate who walks into an OA calm and walks out with a third of the test cases passing on a pattern they "finished" in July.

## 2.4 Solving is three skills; you trained one

| Skill | What it is | Where it's tested | Your state |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Identification | Reading a statement and naming the pattern within 60 seconds | First 5 min of every OA problem | [Untrained — blocked practice removed it]{.red} |
| Construction | Producing the correct template from memory | Minutes 5–15 | [Trained once per problem, then decayed]{.amber} |
| Adaptation | Bending the template to this problem's twist | Minutes 15–30 | [Partially trained]{.amber} |

**Most candidates who fail an OA do not fail on adaptation. They fail on identification — five minutes gone, no idea what kind of problem it is, panic, then a brute force that times out. §8 is the drill that fixes it and it costs forty minutes.**

## 2.5 The reframe

> [!TIP]
> **Stop counting problems. Start counting patterns you can regenerate cold.**
>
> Your target is not 176/176. It is: 19 templates you can blank-page from memory, 39 anchor problems you can cold-solve inside the time bar, and pattern identification within 60 seconds on an unseen statement.
>
> That is a far smaller target than 176 — and unlike 176, it is the one that maps directly onto passing an OA.
